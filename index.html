      Controlled rest on the flight deck — what the research actually says
      
The 40-minute figure everyone quotes is not arbitrary. It comes from a NASA Ames study on long-haul crews, and the numbers are worth knowing because they are the argument for taking the rest rather than pushing through.

      

        
Planned rest
40 min
during low-workload cruise

        
Average time to fall asleep
5.6 min
pilots slept on 93% of opportunities

        
Average sleep obtained
25.8 min
per rest period

        
Recovery before duties
20 min
before resuming or briefing

      

      
What it bought them. Across the in-flight trials the rested group recorded 81 lapses of attention against 124 in the group that did not rest. Microsleeps — brief involuntary sleep intrusions measured on EEG — ran 34 in the rested group against 120 in the group that did not. Crews who had not rested were about twice as likely to have a microsleep during descent and landing, which is precisely where you do not want one.

      
Why 40 minutes and not longer. NASA's own reasoning: "a longer nap increases the possibility that deep sleep will occur and, therefore, might increase the potential effects of sleep inertia." Sleep inertia is the grogginess on waking — it is worst in the first 10 to 15 minutes and typically clears within 20 to 30 minutes after a short nap, which is exactly what the 20-minute recovery period is for.

      
The operational window. Flight Safety Foundation guidance: controlled rest is used only in the cruise, from top of climb to 30 minutes before planned top of descent, capped at 40 minutes, with a minimum 20-minute recovery in which the resting pilot takes no flight duties and no briefings.

      
Rosekind, M.R. et al. (1994), NASA Technical Memorandum 108839, Crew Factors in Flight Operations IX: Effects of Planned Cockpit Rest on Crew Performance and Alertness in Long-Haul Operations; Flight Safety Foundation, Controlled Rest on the Flight Deck.

      
Controlled rest is only available where your operator's procedures provide for it. This is the science behind the numbers, not authorisation to use it — check your own FOM.

        How are these numbers calculated?
        
The calculation works backward from your maximum duty-off time, which is your report time plus the operational limit for your crew complement (CBA 12.D.1 — 20:00 for a four-person crew, 22:00 under Article 33, 17:00 for three, and 11:30–15:00 for two by report time).

        
This tab assumes the CBA limit controls. That holds under Part 121 Subpart R (flag all-cargo), where the FAR sets no duty ceiling at all. It does not hold under Subpart S, where §121.505(b), §121.507(b) and §121.509(b) set 16, 18 and 20 hours of duty in any 24 consecutive hours, or under the §121.513 election, where §121.523(c) caps continuous duty at 30 hours. If you are flying supplemental, check the FAR figure too — whichever is shorter controls (Art. 12.D).

        
From maximum duty-off, subtract 30 minutes of post-flight duty to get your latest block-in, then subtract 10 minutes of taxi-in to get your latest touchdown. Subtract the OFP flight time from that touchdown to get your LATT (Latest Allowable Takeoff Time). Finally, subtract the OFP taxi-out time from the LATT to get your LABT (Latest Allowable Block-out Time).

        
This logic was verified step for step against the worked example in the IAP 2750 News Wire, Issue 221 (January 17, 2025), under “Legal to Start, Legal to Finish: Not at This Airline.”

        What do I do if I can't complete the flight within duty limits?
        
Coordinate with company ground personnel to inform them that the crew has run out of duty day to complete the flight. They should begin the process of coordinating with operations in GCC. Follow up with scheduling and inform them that your duty day will exceed the operational limit given the circumstances.

        
If a crew scheduler or a duty pilot attempts to convince you otherwise, kindly inform them that your limits are set by the CBA, which you wish to exercise. Additionally, you can reach out to the union Crew Desk to connect you to a steward.

        Who must be catered (Art. 5.E)
        
Catering is provided for all operating and deadheading Crew Members, and for jumpseaters who listed at least 12 hours in advance. The Company has no obligation to cater commercial deadheads or ground transportation legs.

        
Meals should endeavor to be American/Western style unless otherwise approved by the Union Catering Committee.

        
Choices: a minimum of two (2) meal choices when catering is required; a minimum of three (3) for augmented crews or when deadheading Crew Members are travelling.

        
Rotation: sandwiches and meal choices rotate approximately every two weeks.

        B737 hot meal alternative (MOU)
        
The B737 has no onboard ovens, so the Company is structurally unable to provide hot meals. On any B737 flight with scheduled block time of four (4) or more hours where a Crew Member would otherwise be entitled to a hot meal, they instead receive a gross payment of US $75.00 per meal.

        
The payment appears on your paycheck and is fully grossed up by the Company to cover associated taxes.

      

      

        Inoperative oven — Arbitration Award #AAI-2025-183
        
If an oven is inoperative on a flight where hot meals are contractually required, there is no automatic contract relief. The Company must remedy in this strict priority order:

        
    
          
  1. Provision a large meal-sized salad or sandwich (or breakfast equivalent) — from a catering vendor first, then a local restaurant.
    2. 
          
  2. Provision a pre-cooked hot meal from a catering vendor.
    3. 
          
  3. If mutually agreeable, authorize the pilot to purchase a meal on the Company credit card (or direct reimbursement).
    4. 
          
  4. Provision a hot meal from a local restaurant.
    5. 
          
  5. Provide a $100.00 meal voucher.
    6. 
        

        
Pilot's option: a pilot may unilaterally elect to bypass the priority list and take a $50.00 meal voucher in lieu of the remedies above.

      

      

        EHU (Ezhou) local catering procedures
        
EHU has only one local caterer, serving non-compliant meals, so alternative procedures were established:

        
Through catering: where aircraft space, ground time and combined block times allow, catering is loaded at the prior station and labeled by leg.

        
Hotel carry-on catering: when through-catering is impossible, place a food order with the front desk of your primary hotel (Wanda Hotel Huangshi or Crowne Plaza Ezhou) at least 16 hours prior to departure. The hotel prepares meals cold in oven-safe tins; the crew member carries them to the aircraft to reheat and charges the cost to their card/room (pre-authorized by accounting).

      

      

        Safety & enforcement
        
Unsecured galley items: all catering, including water cases and coolers, must be properly stowed and secured in galley cabinets and bins prior to departure. Loose items on the galley or flight deck floor are a severe safety hazard and violate FAA regulations and FOM policy.

        
No obligation to depart: operating crews, and Captains in particular, are under no obligation to depart until contractually compliant, safe and sanitary catering is on board.

        
Voucher refusal: outside the inoperative-oven award, the union strongly advises never accepting or negotiating a voucher in lieu of contractually required food. Captains cannot unilaterally accept vouchers on behalf of their crew.

⚠️ Planning aid — not a measurement, not a legality check

        
This predicts alertness from what you enter. It cannot know the sleep you actually got. Use it to plan rest and spot risk, never as proof you are fit — that judgement is always yours.

        
Legal is not the same as safe. Duty legality lives in the LABT/LATT and Schedule Checks tabs. Under Part 121 Subpart R (flag all-cargo) there is no maximum duty period at all — only flight-time caps — so there your CBA is the only duty backstop. Subpart S does set duty ceilings; pick the regime below and the app will apply the right ones. Either way, a schedule can be entirely legal and still unflyable.

          Where do I get this file? (step by step)
          
    
            
  1. On a computer or phone browser, go to aims.atlasair.com/eCrew and log in with your normal eCrew credentials.
    2. 
            
  2. Open My Schedule.
    3. 
            
  3. Set the date range you want — one month at a time is easiest.
    4. 
            
  4. Choose UTC for the times. Local Station also works, but UTC is unambiguous — see the note below.
    5. 
            
  5. Click PRINT.
    6. 
            
  6. Save the file as CSV or XLS — both are read. On a phone eCrew usually only offers XLS, and that is fine: the XLS is actually the better file (see below).
    7. 
            
  7. Come back here and tap Upload schedule above. You can select several files at once, or add them one at a time.
    8. 
          

          
Covering more than one month: every upload adds to what is already stored — nothing is replaced. Export each month, upload them all, and the fatigue model runs continuously across the whole span, carrying your sleep debt and body clock from one month into the next. Re-uploading a month you already have just refreshes it rather than duplicating it. The stored span is shown under the button, with a Clear control if you want to start over.

          
Why XLS is better: the CSV export is written in a character set that cannot hold the superscript +1 / −1 day markers, so both come out as the same character and a westbound date-line arrival is indistinguishable from an eastbound one. The XLS keeps the sign. The app resolves the CSV case by picking the reading that yields a believable block time, but the XLS never has to guess. The XLS also carries your hotels, which the CSV omits entirely.

          
UTC or Local Station? Both are supported — the export writes which one it used on line 2 of the file, so the app reads it and never has to guess. UTC is recommended: on duties that cross midnight or the date line, the Local export dates the row in a way that can be ambiguous, and UTC removes that entirely.

          
What gets read: report and debrief times, block out/in, stations, flight numbers, aircraft, deadhead legs, and OFF/GDO days. Multi-leg duty days are split into separate legs automatically. From an XLS the app also reads the hotel for each layover and shows it on the matching rest period.

          
Privacy: the file is read on your device only and never uploaded anywhere. Note the export also contains your crewmates' names and employee numbers — that stays on your phone, but bear it in mind before sharing the file itself.

    2. 
          
  2. Quality and recency of my sleep?
    3. 
          
  3. How long will my duty continue?
    4. 
          
  4. How much longer will I be awake?
    5. 
          
  5. What is my workload — maintenance, weather, delays?
    6. 
        

        

          
The 4-hour rule of thumb

          
If you have had fewer than 4 hours of sleep in the previous 24, consider yourself susceptible to fatigue regardless of what any model says.

        

        
Reporting is not the same as calling out. In a mature FRMS you can file a fatigue report to document a schedule that produced fatigue without removing yourself from the trip. Those reports are what drive scheduling changes. Check what your own contract and company programme provide.

        
Model honesty: fatigue modelling assumes an ideal scenario. It cannot know about a 3-hour maintenance delay, a diversion, or a hotel with a fire alarm at 0300. The same pairing affects different pilots differently, and the same pilot differently on different days. Treat the score as one input, never the verdict.

        What do these terms mean?
        
Release check — a schedule check due 30 minutes after you block in, as you're released from the duty you just flew.

        
Minimum Rest Period — the contractual rest that begins right after you block in. You have no contactability during it.

        
Layover Period — your free time between the end of Minimum Rest and the start of Pre-Duty Rest. Ad-Hoc/Secondary/Reserve may owe 12-hr checks here.

        
Pre-Duty Rest Period — the 10-hour period right before your next report time. Your “10-hr pre-duty check” is due as it begins (report − 10:00).

        
32/168 — you must be given ≥ 32 consecutive hours free of all duty within any 168 hours before a duty period. A schedule check counts as duty, so some checks are waived to protect it.
